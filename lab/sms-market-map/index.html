Route

The text passes through one or more wholesale hubs before the operator. More reach, more hops. Gold marks arrival.

Typically 160 GSM-7 or 70 UCS-2 units fit in a single SMS (Chinese text uses UCS-2). When a message is split, each part holds 153 or 67 units, and each part is billed.

The chain, in order

  1. Business the sender

    A bank, a shop, an app, an airline or a public body. It pays for the message, but what it really buys is an outcome: a finished login, a traveller who knows the gate has changed.

  2. Software partners CRM · SaaS · integrators

    Marketing and CRM tools, cloud platforms, systems integrators and resellers build messaging into software the business already uses. Many businesses never talk to a mobile operator at all.

  3. Messaging provider CPaaS · API · SMPP

    Offers an API or SMPP connection, chooses routes, returns delivery reports, helps register sender IDs and templates, and sends the invoice. Most of the buying decision happens here.

  4. Aggregators wholesale hubs

    Buy connections to many operators and resell them wholesale. Each extra hop adds reach — and a margin, a little latency and less visibility into where the message really went.

  5. SIM farm grey route

    Some low-cost routes push business traffic through consumer SIM cards or unauthorised interconnects, disguised as person-to-person texts. It looks cheap; operator firewalls block it, delivery turns erratic, and some such routes return delivery reports that are not genuine.

  6. Mobile operator delivers · sets the rules

    Delivers the message on its own network and is paid a termination fee. Operators and regulators set the rules — registration of sender IDs and templates, consent for marketing — and run SMS firewalls against spam and fraud.

  7. Phone the recipient

    The only place where ‘delivered’ is certain. Serious tests put real handsets on the destination networks and compare what arrives with what the delivery reports claim.

What does ‘delivered’ mean?

Five different numbers hide behind one word. A contract should say which one it pays for.

  1. Accepted by the APIThe provider took the request. Acceptance alone doesn’t show whether it has reached the network or the phone.waiting
  2. Submitted to the operatorHanded to the network — or to the next hop.waiting
  3. Delivery report says ‘delivered’A status report came back. It is only as honest as every hop.waiting
  4. Seen on a real phoneChecked on a test handset. Costly, so it is sampled.waiting
  5. The business outcomeThe login completed; the parcel was collected. This is what the customer bought.waiting

What they all share

The shared job

Get the critical message to the right people on time — and prove it.

How they buy

A technical test, a commercial comparison and a cross-team review; tuning today’s routes while opening new markets.

02

What they need

Stated needs, and the ones nobody writes down.

Stated needs

It arrives, on time

The right destination and operator; latency, success criteria and peak load tested for each use case.

Easy to connect, easy to see

API or SMPP, status callbacks, error codes, reports and message tracing.

Predictable cost and delivery

Quote validity, per-segment billing, incident handling, invoices, advance notice of route changes.

Unstated needs

Outcomes they can count on

Fewer abandoned logins, missed notices and failed deliveries; for marketing, real extra sales.

Less organisational risk

No complaints, misfires, budget overruns or failed switch-overs; less dependence on one supplier.

Less coordination

One contact for technical, commercial, upstream and support; when something breaks, someone owns it.

05

Why they stay

What wins the first order, and what wins the renewal.

The first order hinges on

Evidence of quality

Delivery reports, handset spot checks, latency and failure reasons — never a single ‘success rate’.

Integration and risk control

Documentation, sender-ID and template registration, permissions, incident plans.

True total cost

Segment billing, charges for failed messages, minimum commitments, payment terms, disputes.

Renewal and growth hinge on

Stability and response

Is quality sustained? Are incidents found, fixed and reviewed quickly?

Billing and teamwork

Can invoices be checked? Are route changes announced in advance? Is support reliable?

Room to grow

Can new countries, use cases and volumes be proven? Can a switch be rolled back?

The trade in one lineCustomers don’t buy message counts. They buy delivery they can verify, risk they can control, and a total cost they can sustain.

Fig. 3Definitions

Delivered is not done

Three kinds of message, three definitions of success — and the objection each one answers.

Try: compare Passcodes with Opt-in marketing.

01 · The business outcome

Finish the verification — not just send the code.

Less waiting and drop-off in sign-up, login and payment checks, judged on real verification sessions.

02 · Metric and denominator

Verification completion rate

unique sessions verified within the code’s validity divided by valid sessions in which a code was sent

Resends do not create new sessions; abnormal requests and pre-send failures are listed separately. This is not the whole sign-up funnel. Delivery-report latency and handset arrival latency are measured separately.

03 · Objection → evidence

“Your success rate is so high — so why can’t our users log in?”

Line up session IDs, message IDs, submit and delivery-report times and verification results; split failures into network delivery, timeouts, code entry and the app’s own errors.

Transactional alerts

01 · The business outcome

Tell the right person about the change, in time.

Orders, flight changes, appointments and payment alerts: fewer missed, duplicated or chased-up notices.

02 · Metric and denominator

Critical notices on time

event–recipient pairs that met the time limit divided by event–recipient pairs that should have been notified

Count each ‘business event × intended recipient’ pair once; retries and segments never count twice; unknown or timed-out pairs stay in the denominator, listed separately. With handset sampling, both come from the same sample, and results do not extrapolate to all traffic or to ‘read’.

03 · Objection → evidence

“The invoice says everything was sent — so why is customer service getting complaints about missing notices?”

Reconcile send logs, recipient mapping, templates, segment completeness and timing against business events; add delivery reports and handset spot checks. ‘Submitted’ does not close the case.

Opt-in marketing

01 · The business outcome

Create extra value — not extra volume.

Within audiences who properly opted in, measure the extra conversions a campaign causes, while watching opt-outs, complaints and message fatigue.

02 · Metric and denominator

Incremental conversion

conversion rate of the randomly assigned send group− minus conversion rate of the concurrent control group

Both rates use everyone originally assigned to each group; undelivered messages stay in their group; report the difference in percentage points. Agree audience, window and exclusions in advance. Without a control group, report association only — not lift.

03 · Objection → evidence

“Cheaper per message and more clicks — so we should renew, right?”

Check incremental conversion, segment and other agreed fees, opt-outs and complaints together. Do not credit SMS with conversions that would have happened anyway, or with every last click.

Suggested definitions; no target values are given. Before a test, agree the sample, time window, exclusions and denominator, and keep supplier delivery reports and business events as separate evidence.

Before the price

Six questions to ask before anyone quotes a price per message

A useful first conversation settles these first. The checklist carries the buyer and the kind of message you picked above.

  1. 01
    Why look for a new supplier now?

    A new market, an outage, a contract ending, or cost pressure?

  2. 02
    Who exactly are you sending to, and what?

    Destination countries and operators, message type, language and sender.

  3. 03
    When, and how much?

    Daily volume, peak hours, throughput and the go-live window.

  4. 04
    What result counts as success?

    Agree submission, delivery reports, handset checks and business results separately.

  5. 05
    Who evaluates, who approves, who pays?

    Business, engineering, security, procurement, legal and the budget holder.

  6. 06
    What would make you stay?

    Sustained quality, total cost, responsiveness, reconciliation and room to grow.

Words on this page

A2P SMS
Application-to-person: texts sent by a business system to people — codes, alerts, marketing — as opposed to person-to-person chat.
OTP
A one-time passcode, sent to verify a login or a payment.
CPaaS
Communications platform as a service: a provider that sells messaging through APIs.
SMPP
Short Message Peer-to-Peer, the standard protocol providers and operators use to exchange SMS.
Delivery report (DLR)
A status report sent back after a message is submitted. It is not proof that anyone read it.
Sender ID
The name or number shown as the sender. Many countries require it to be registered.
Segment
The billing unit: typically 160 GSM-7 or 70 UCS-2 units in a single SMS; 153 / 67 per part when a message is split.
Grey route
Delivery through channels the receiving operator has not authorised, such as SIM farms.
SMS firewall
Operator filtering that blocks spam, fraud and unauthorised business traffic.
NOC
Network operations centre: the team that watches routes and fixes incidents.

About this map

A framework, not a survey. Buyer types, needs and decision roles are analytical hypotheses drawn from common industry practice — not interview findings, a customer list or anyone’s revenue mix.

The page names no clients, suppliers or prices, and makes no claim about coverage, quality or service levels. The first version was drawn in September 2026 as a working map for a messaging sales team; this version keeps its logic and removes everything company-specific.
