How a shortcut splits an arrival

Four small experiments on the same ring. Each works on its own; the step numbers jump straight to any of them.

Steps1234

  1. 1

    One walker

    One walker on a ring of 120 sites. Each tick it rests (probability q = 0.2) or steps to a neighbour. The first time it touches the target — either way round — is its first-passage time. Release a few more: the times scatter wildly.

  2. 2

    Many walkers

    Release a crowd and histogram their arrival times. As the crowd grows the bars settle onto one smooth curve — the exact answer, from evolving the whole probability distribution (the master equation) instead of sampling it. One target, one peak.

  3. 3

    Add a shortcut

    Now add a chord: a walker standing at u jumps to v with probability β each tick. Walkers that find the chord early arrive almost at once; the rest still go the long way round. The same single target now produces two peaks — as long as β stays small.

  4. 4

    Sweep β

    Turn β up slowly, or press Play the sweep. The shortcut peak rises out of the floor; past a critical β the round-the-ring peak runs into the valley and vanishes — a maximum and a minimum collide and annihilate: a saddle-node fold.

The record

A random walker wanders until it hits a target. How long that takes — the first-passage time — is usually one hump. Add one directed shortcut, and it can split in two: a fast arrival and a slow one, with a valley between.

It began with an MSc dissertation on walkers trapped in boxes. Rings came first, then two-dimensional lattices, then pairs of particles that react when they meet — each result public as it arrived, in a bilingual research atlas. By the summer the double peak was a theory, checked five ways; then came a second line, in which a fixed budget of catalyst programs how many peaks appear.

Why it matters

First-passage times are everywhere a search has to succeed: a molecule finding a receptor, a signal crossing a network, a pest finding a trap (Chapter 07).

Why a second peak is born — and dies

The shortcut removes walkers at one site (a ‘rank-one killing defect’); at strength ≈ 3.08 the late peak meets a valley and both vanish.

The ring’s double peak became a threshold theory. Zoom out until steps blur (the diffusive limit) and the walk becomes Brownian motion — continuous random movement — on an interval, with a sink inside it standing for the shortcut. The first-passage density is then a signed mixture of decay modes, and a second peak needs at least three. With the sink at the midpoint, the fold comes at a sink strength of about 3.0764, with exponents ½ and ³⁄₂. Five independent checks agree, among them exact lattice residues and a random-simulation (Monte Carlo) check with 400,000 walkers.

The term that cancels

9–11 Jun 2026 Completed

For a lazy ring with an antipodal shortcut, a ‘t·αᵗ’ term was suspected in the long-time tail of the first-passage distribution. It is not there. Three independent arguments show that its coefficient is identically zero: a partial-fraction sum rule; a trace of the term to an artefact of the generating-function inversion; and symmetry — the transient matrix is diagonalisable, so terms like t·λᵗ cannot occur for any parameters. Every identity was checked in 50-digit arithmetic and with exact rational kernels.

46 theorems, checked by machine

5 Jul 2026 Completed

The exact algebra of the fold paper — from Chebyshev product identities and the rank-one determinant to the closed-form splitting probability and the minimal three-mode theorem — was formalised in Lean 4, a proof assistant, with its mathlib library: 46 theorems across seven modules, with no ‘sorry’ (Lean’s placeholder for a missing proof) and only Lean’s three standard axioms. Numerical constants and analytic limits were left out of scope on purpose and checked numerically instead.
